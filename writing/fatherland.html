Fatherland
Loving Past and Present

At a press conference during the beginning of Paweł Pawlikowski’s film Fatherland, Thomas Mann is asked whether capitalism or communism is the best way forward for humanity. “Neither is sufficient in itself,” he responds, “a good society should be shaped in the image of man: not the other way around.” Mann, paraphrasing Kant, expands that “from such crooked timber as mankind nothing quite straight can ever be fashioned.” It is this vision of a society shaped around the human being which Mann puts forward, against the Stalinist drive to produce the new man and capitalist techniques of mass consumer standardization, at two separate ceremonies in East and West Germany where he is to receive a Goethe prize. If society is meant to be formed around man, however, then one must have a view as to who man is. The film’s beginning leaves this question in suspense, concluding the press conference with Mann being asked whether Germany or America, his fatherland or adoptive country, is his home. He says that he is a German writer and an American citizen, but the question as to where he belongs is more difficult.

The first speech delivered in West Germany centers around what Mann sees in Goethe as life affirmation. In this Mann is trying to clear a path for a “Good Germany” of the future from within the cultural tradition of Germany itself which he distinguishes from the Germanic, romantic death cult that culminated in Hitler. Goethe, according to Mann, accomplishes this through his affirmation of life which is concretized in love. Mann defines this life-affirming love as the love of what lives and the mourning of the dead, without leaving the dead behind. Juxtaposed against what Mann identifies as “our illness” which is an “unwillingness to face the challenges of life”, this life-affirmatory love is a sisterly relation to life.1 When in the East, Mann builds on the Western speech stating that Goethe was a poet with an open gaze who could see the contradictions in life of good and evil. Furthermore, this open gaze revealed to Goethe life’s fundamental tragedy which is that the realization of the idea is its corruption. We can thus see in the second speech a critique against the emerging societies for their fabrication of man from a blind, dialectically unaware standpoint and in the first an outline of the proper attunement of man towards life around which society should be established.

Mann’s speeches, however, do not merely consist in an abstract outline for a society of the future but are concertized in the historical circumstance of both Germany and Mann himself. In the film it is mentioned that as there is a dispute about Mann the writer there is a dispute about Germany the nation. While Germany is ensnared between “two ideas” for its future, Mann himself is subject to the attempt at his being appropriated for the purposes of the Western and Eastern blocs. Johannes Becher tries to entice Mann to become a symbol in the communist world by taking a position at a university. Western media begins linking Mann with left-wing intellectuals and artists due to his accepting a Goethe award in the GDR while Erika points out that Mann must go to the East as well so as not to appear to be a “poodle” of the “yankees”. Klaus and Erika are aware of this state of affairs evidenced when the former notes that the Germans just want his father to come in order to clear their consciences and the latter observes that her father’s speeches are useless and that he has been rendered an ornament. When Erika confronts her father with this, saying the Russians and Americans run the show and that his “Olympian phrases” are merely background music, Mann reasserts the importance of his speeches and, incidentally, confirms Erika’s suspicion that her father believes himself to occupy a standpoint above the historical-political antagonisms of the moment. From this Goethean open gaze, Mann asserts that “if fate wills that our existence become symbolic, we must face it.” As Germany in itself symbolizes the fate of Cold War Europe, Mann believes he can espy the politico-historical contradictions dividing his fatherland. Mann even uses the phrase “Here I stand” quoting the iconic dictum used by Luther: paralleling himself with this world-historical thinker who remade what would become Germany at another seminal moment of division. He is thereby convinced that he can chart an escape from this polarity dividing Germany through his vision of a new society which should be realized in his fatherland and could be an example to mankind as a whole.

Whereas Erika and Klaus could immediately perceive their father’s being appropriated, the journey Mann undergoes in the film is his realization of this fact. In the West, instead of encountering an eager and enthusiastic reception of his ideas, Mann is met with a flashy afterparty following his speech in which Wagner’s grandsons ask him for favors. In the East, a Soviet general at his dinner party attempts to reduce the substance of Mann’s argument to a poetical re-expression of historical materialism, of which Mann remains unconvinced. Mann’s attempt to speak into existence a society where man could finally belong, rather than be subject to a Procrustean fabrication, falls entirely on deaf ears. However, in his daughter’s confrontation with him, it is made clear that Mann himself had fallen victim to the illness of the times by not confronting the challenges of life as he has not been living in accordance with the life-affirming love which he espouses for humanity. Instead of mourning Klaus, Mann blindly pushes ahead with his speeches in order to, as Erika discerns, solidify his legacy. This obsession with legacy is confirmed in, for example, his remarks about where Bach was a Kapellmeister and his visiting Goethe’s deathbed and seeing his deathmask.2 Throughout his legacy-cementing journey in post-war Germany, his mind has been turned towards the endurance of his renown and the legends who preceded him. Mann has been living entirely in the future tense, striving to secure a legacy by exhorting the birth of a new Germany, running in contradistinction to the very life-affirming love Mann emphasizes: requiring a love of the living now and mourning for those past.

The crux on which the film hinges is the relationship of Mann and Erika to Klaus disclosed through Klaus’s suicide. Whereas Erika, whose special relationship with Klaus had been emphasized from the film’s first scene when on the phone with each other Klaus says “it used to be us against the world”, immediately enters into a deep mourning for her brother, revealing why the film noted that authentic Goethean love is sisterly, Mann seems unperturbed by the death of his son. This dissonance explodes in the confrontation between daughter and father when Erika asks how he can go on with the trip. Mann initially attempts to distance himself from Klaus saying that what he is doing now involves things at stake which are much more important than the fate of a drug addicted son. However, following a revelatory, mystical dream where he stands over a casket and sees Klaus among the mourners, Mann turns to his son. Near the film’s end, Mann finds Erika bereaved in bed. She tells him that “I see Klaus everywhere” to which Mann responds “I dreamt of him.” Erika, looking at her father knowing that their grief is now shared, says “Let’s go home” to which Mann wonders “Where is that?” The answer to this question is not spoken but imparted in the film’s final scene.

While driving through East Germany Mann switches between American and Communist stations before turning the radio off. The film opened with Klaus quoting an interview in which his father is asked, “Mr. Mann, which side are you on? Stalin or Mickey Mouse?” As has been noted, Mann saw insufficiencies in both impelling him towards his humanity-centered vision of society. However, while Mann began the film believing he could transform his historical-political situation through expressing this vision, he now rejects his political engagement as a futile, vain distraction from realizing life-affirming love within his own life. Having turned off the radio, Mann and Erika drive away from their escort and enter a church. When they first came to East Germany, Erika noted that Bach was the only composer Klaus listened to in the end and that Bach “almost made him believe in God.” This is a key detail as during Klaus’s nihilistic phone call with Erika he says that he believes in nothing anymore (especially not culture or art from which Mann trusts a civilized “Good Germany” can emerge). Mann had then attempted to estrange himself from his son saying “I thought Nietzsche said that about Beethoven.” It seems Mann, by suggesting that Erika’s statement about Klaus’s love for Bach is actually derivative of the romantic philosopher Nietzsche’s adoration for the romantic composer Beethoven, is associating his son with the romantic German death cult he abhors and which would appear to be vindicated in Klaus’s suicide. However, when Mann enters the church the organist happens to be playing Bach’s Jesus bleibt meine freude. Upon hearing this Mann, stolid up to now, weeps and holds his daughter’s hand. Having abandoned his vainglorious political engagement and the future-oriented obsession for his own legacy on which it was based, Mann has been brought to authentically incarnate the Gothean conception of life-affirming love which he professed: loving his living daughter Erika in the present and mourning his dead son Klaus. Thus the search for belonging has ended. Mann found where he belonged not in a nation or ideology but in the very home-creating love his speeches recognized but now turned away from society, history, or politics and towards his daughter and son.

1 In East Germany Mann will hear what is presented to him as the new anthem of the GDR which stresses love of the fatherland and fraternity among its united citizens, common themes throughout European political thought.
